Your shower has the enthusiasm of a garden hose someone is standing on, and you've started negotiating with it. Bad news: the shower isn't listening. Good news: low water pressure is one of the most fixable problems in plumbing, and you can often sort it out yourself before you ever need a **plumber for low water pressure**.

Let's figure out which camp you're in — the lucky "two-minute fix" camp or the "okay, this one's ours" camp — in about ten minutes.

## First, is it one faucet or the whole house?

This single question splits the entire problem in half, so start here.

- **One faucet or shower:** the problem is *local* — almost always a clogged aerator or showerhead. Cheap and easy.
- **The whole house:** the problem is *systemic* — a main valve, a pressure regulator, a leak, or the city. Bigger, but still often DIY-able.

Go turn on a few taps around the house. We'll wait. (We've got nothing but time and a deep, professional love of water pressure.)

## If it's just one faucet

Most likely culprit: the **aerator**, that little mesh screen at the tip of the faucet. Minerals clog it like cholesterol in an artery.

1. Unscrew the aerator by hand (or with pliers wrapped in tape so you don't scratch it).
2. Rinse the screen, then soak it in white vinegar for an hour to dissolve mineral buildup.
3. Screw it back on and test.

Showerhead doing the same thing? Same fix — unscrew it and give it a vinegar bath. This is the single most common "low pressure" call we get, and it costs you a splash of vinegar.

## If it's the whole house

Now we work through the usual suspects, cheapest first:

### 1. The main shutoff valve isn't all the way open
If someone worked on your plumbing recently, the main valve may have been left half-closed. Find it (usually where the water line enters the house) and make sure it's fully open. This is free, takes 30 seconds, and fixes more "mystery" pressure problems than anything else.

### 2. A failing pressure regulator
This is the bell-shaped valve near your main line. When it fails, pressure across the whole house drops (or spikes). Testing it means putting a gauge on an outdoor spigot — normal is [**40–60 psi**](https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/water-qa-water-pressure). A bad regulator is a real repair, and one we'd handle as part of our [plumbing repair service](/services/plumbing-services), since it often shows up alongside other aging valves.

### 3. A hidden leak
If pressure is dropping *and* your water bill is climbing, you may have a leak quietly feeding a wall or a slab. This is the one that costs you if you ignore it. We once caught a pinhole leak in a copper line that had been weeping behind drywall for months — a faint ceiling stain was the only clue. If you suspect this, don't wait it out.

### 4. It's not you, it's the city
Sometimes the utility is flushing lines or doing repairs and pressure dips for a day. A quick call to your water provider rules this out. The U.S. EPA has a good plain-English primer on [household water use and supply](https://www.epa.gov/watersense) if you want the background.

## When to actually call a plumber

Call us (or any licensed plumber) when:

- Pressure is low everywhere **and** the main valve is fully open.
- Your pressure gauge reads under 40 psi or bounces around.
- Pressure is dropping over days and your bill is creeping up (possible leak).
- You've got galvanized steel pipes from the disco era — corrosion narrows them from the inside, and that's a [repipe conversation](/services), not a DIY one.
