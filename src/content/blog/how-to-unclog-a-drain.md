Nobody plans their evening around standing water in the sink, and yet here you are, watching it drain at the pace of a glacier with a grudge. The good news: learning **how to unclog a drain** takes about five minutes, costs almost nothing, and rarely requires a professional — as long as you skip the one "solution" that does more harm than good.

Let's clear it the right way.

## Start with the right tool (it's not a chemical)

Before anything, reach for a **plunger** or a cheap **plastic drain-zip strip**, not a bottle of caustic goo. Here's the order of operations that clears most clogs:

1. **Bathroom sink/tub (usually hair):** push the plastic zip strip down, twist, and pull. Brace yourself — what comes up is genuinely horrifying, and that's the clog gone.
2. **Kitchen sink (usually grease + food):** use a flat-bottomed cup plunger. Block the overflow or the second basin, get a tight seal, and plunge hard 10–15 times.
3. **Still slow:** move to a hand snake (drain auger) and feed it down until you feel the blockage, then crank through it.

## The home remedies worth trying

The internet's favorite is **baking soda and vinegar**, and it's fine — for minor, gunky slowdowns. Pour half a cup of baking soda down the drain, chase it with a cup of white vinegar, let it fizz for 15 minutes, then flush with a kettle of hot (not boiling, if you have PVC) water. It dissolves light soap scum and freshens things up.

What it *won't* do is blow through a solid hair plug or a congealed grease clog. If the fizz-and-flush doesn't fix it, you've got a real clog and it needs a tool, not chemistry.

## The thing to never pour down the drain

Here's our hill to die on: **skip liquid drain cleaners.** They eat older pipes from the inside, frequently don't clear the actual blockage, and — this is the part people don't know — they turn the standing water into a caustic hazard for whoever snakes the line next. A snake or a camera beats a bottle of acid every single time. (If you do have a half-used bottle, don't pour it out — the EPA's [household hazardous waste guidance](https://www.epa.gov/hw/household-hazardous-waste-hhw) covers disposing of it safely.)

And in the kitchen specifically, the clog usually starts with grease. The EPA has a good plain-English explainer on why you should [never pour fats, oils, or grease down the drain](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2022-01/fog-fact-sheet.pdf) — let it cool, scrape it in the trash, and your drains will thank you for years.

## When a slow drain means something bigger

One slow drain is usually local. But call a pro when:

- **Several fixtures back up at once** (sink gurgles when the toilet flushes).
- Water rises in one drain when you run a *different* one.
- You snake it clear and it clogs again within days.
